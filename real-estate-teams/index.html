The problem in one number

Nobody on your team is ignoring leads on purpose. They’re at showings.

In Mike DelPrete’s August 2024 secret shop of 100+ shops across 25+ brokerages, 47% of online property inquiries got no response at all, and the median first reply took 39 minutes. Buyers and sellers act on that gap: 47% of buyers and 59% of sellers hired the first agent they actually spoke with (Zillow, December 2025).

Sources: Mike DelPrete secret shop, August 2024; Zillow Consumer Housing Trends Report for Agents, December 2025. The two 47% figures come from different studies: the first is inquiries with no reply, the second is buyers who hired the first agent they spoke with.

Why teach it at all

63% of agents call the learning curve their biggest tech challenge (NAR 2026 Technology Report, 1,165 responses). A tool nobody on the team learned to use is one more subscription. So the sessions are short, they’re spaced out, and each one ends with homework on fictional leads, so nobody practices on a real client.

What happens in each session

  1. Before session 1

    Preflight call or form

    Before session 1, a short preflight call or form. I confirm that someone with admin access to your CRM will be in the room, whether your texting registration is approved, which lead sources create new-lead events, what automations already exist that could fire twice, and who gets alerted. I also preload your team’s AI Project and draft the templates from your intake answers, so live time goes to setup and practice.

  2. Session 1

    30 minutes. Set it up.

    We read your CRM’s response-time and unanswered-lead numbers together, write down your business hours and exceptions, and name the one person accountable. You approve the starter templates, each one run through the fair-housing check. Then we load one lead source and one routing rule, send a test lead, and confirm the first message fires.

    Homework: each agent answers fictional leads in the team’s Project.

  3. Session 2

    30 minutes. Practice.

    We review the homework, then run timed drills on three kinds of lead: a hot buyer, a vague portal lead and a seller. A couple of the practice leads are built to tempt a fair-housing slip, on purpose. We agree the handoff rule: when a person calls instead of texting.

    Homework: run it on real inquiries this week.

  4. Session 3

    30 minutes. Scoreboard.

    We read three numbers against your baseline: did the automatic first message fire, how long until a person made a first attempt, and did a person complete the handoff. We fix the weakest templates, set a Monday ritual, and book your day-30 review.

What you leave with

  • A set of approved templates: a first text for each lead source, a first email and a short follow-up plan
  • A Project in your own ChatGPT or Claude account that holds your team’s voice, service area and rules
  • A fair-housing check prompt and a short human checklist
  • A handoff rule everyone agreed to out loud
  • A baseline and a three-number scoreboard read from your own dashboard
  • A one-page checklist, the session transcripts and notes, and one bounded day-30 review

You own all of it on day 31. Nothing is hosted, and nothing renews.

What if my team has never used AI?

That’s the normal starting point. 63% of agents call the learning curve their biggest tech challenge (NAR 2026 Technology Report), so each session is short and ends with homework. Will AI-written texts get us in trouble with fair housing?

They can, which is why nothing goes out on the AI’s say-so. Every template runs through a fair-housing check prompt, a person on your team approves it, and your broker-in-charge sets your standard. A 2026 Delta survey of about 100 brokerage leaders found 97% say their agents use AI, and the guardrails averaged 6 out of 10 (Florida Realtors coverage). I’m not a lawyer, and this isn’t legal advice. The sources in the check are North Carolina’s, so your broker sets the list for your state.
