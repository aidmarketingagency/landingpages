The AI Follow-Up Map: A 24-Hour Playbook for Contractor Leads

Speed is the single biggest variable in whether a contractor lead books or goes cold. Responding within 5 minutes of a homeowner's inquiry makes a lead dramatically more likely to qualify, and the first 24 hours determine whether that lead turns into a booked job or a lost opportunity. This playbook breaks down exactly what to text, call, and email at each hour so no lead falls through the cracks.

Why the 5-Minute Rule Still Decides Who Books the Job

The math is brutal. When a homeowner searches for a roofer or an HVAC tech, they are in a state of immediate need. Older research from the MIT-affiliated Lead Response Management Study found that contacting a web lead within 5 minutes made it 21 times more likely to be qualified than waiting even 30 minutes (MIT/InsideSales, 2007). That number is from 2007, but the behavior it describes has not budged. Recent contractor-platform data confirms that speed-to-lead remains the single largest conversion variable in home services (Housecall Pro, 2025).

When a homeowner submits a form at 9 PM or calls during a storm, they want to talk to someone now. Not in an hour. Not after lunch. The contractor who picks up or texts back first wins the job more often than the one with the best reviews or the lowest price. If you wait, you are just another tab in their browser, and the next contractor on the list gets the call.

The Reality Gap: What Homeowners Expect vs. What Contractors Deliver

The gap between homeowner expectations and contractor response times is where money leaks. Homeowners expect a response within an hour, but many home-service businesses still take hours or even days to reply (Angi, 2025).

The delay is rarely about laziness. If you are on a roof or under a house, you physically cannot stop to type a personalized email or dial a new lead. But the market does not care about your current job site. Homeowners equate a slow response with a lack of professionalism. When you fail to reply within the first hour, the lead cools down, they start comparing prices, and they move on to whoever was faster.

Invoca call intelligence data shows that a significant share of inbound calls to home-service businesses go unanswered during business hours, and those calls rarely come back (Invoca, 2025). Every missed call is a lead that walked into your store, looked around, and left because nobody acknowledged them.

The 24-Hour Follow-Up Map: What to Send and When

To win the lead, a multi-channel approach beats a single phone call. Here is the cadence that works for the first 24 hours.

Minutes 0 to 5: The Instant Text

The moment a lead comes in, send an automated text. This confirms receipt and tells the homeowner a real person saw their inquiry. Keep it short and specific: "Hi, this is [Name] with [Company]. I got your request for [service type] and will call you in a few minutes to get details."

Why text first? A text lands in the homeowner's hand instantly, and it does not require them to stop what they are doing to answer a call. The text is the acknowledgment. The call is the booking.

Minutes 5 to 10: The First Call

If the lead provided a phone number, call them now. If they do not answer, leave a brief voicemail. Do not hang up without a message. A voicemail tells the homeowner you are real and you tried. The tone matters. Sound like a person, not a script.

Minute 15: The Confirmation Email

Send a short email with your contact information, a one-line summary of your process, and a link to your reviews or recent project photos. The email creates a paper trail and gives the homeowner a second way to reach you if they prefer email over phone. Some people do. Give them the option.

Hour 4: The Value Check-In

If you have not connected yet, send a second text or email. This is the time to share a project photo, a review, or a quick note about your availability this week. Shift the conversation from "I am waiting for a quote" to "Here is proof the work gets done right."

Hour 24: The Final Attempt

If you still have no response, make one final call. Leave a message saying you are closing their file for now but remain available if they still need help. This "closing the file" message creates urgency and often pulls people back who were planning to call you "later."

What a Slow First Hour Costs a Roofing Shop

The cost of slow follow-up is not a single missed call. It is a compounding pattern that plays out across every lead that comes in while you are on a job.

The MIT study frames the stakes clearly: a lead contacted within 5 minutes is 21 times more likely to qualify than one contacted after 30 minutes (MIT/InsideSales, 2007). Now layer in the Invoca finding that a significant share of inbound calls to home-service businesses go unanswered during business hours (Invoca, 2025). Every one of those unanswered calls is a lead that entered the pipeline at full value and decayed to near-zero before anyone had a chance to respond.

A common pattern in roofing shops: the highest-value calls, storm damage inspections, tend to come in clusters between 4 PM and 7 PM. That is exactly when crews are wrapping up for the day and office staff has gone home. These are not tire-kickers. They are homeowners with a tree branch through their roof who want someone out tomorrow morning. The shop that has an automated text-back firing in those hours captures the inspection. The shop that waits until 8 AM the next day to return calls finds the homeowner already booked with the first contractor who answered. The lead was not lost to a better price. It was lost to a faster reply.

That pattern repeats in every trade. The revenue lost to slow follow-up does not show up as a line item on a P&L. It shows up as jobs that never made it onto the schedule because the homeowner already signed with the contractor who responded first. The leads were there. The phone rang. The opportunity was real. It just went to the shop that picked up.

How AI Bridges the Gap When You Are On a Roof

You cannot be in two places at once. AI-driven text-back systems handle the initial engagement the moment a lead submits a form or calls and gets no answer. The text goes out in seconds, the homeowner feels acknowledged, and you get the buffer you need to finish the job and call them back.

In many shops, this single automation is the difference between a lead that goes cold and a lead that books. A missed-call text-back keeps the door open while you are on the roof or under a sink, and it takes the pressure off the first response entirely.

Beyond Day One: Persistence Over the First Week

The first 24 hours are critical, but they are only the opening round. Lead follow-up is a game of persistence, and the contractors who win are the ones who follow up more times, not fewer.

If a lead does not respond on day one, do not assume it is dead. Homeowners are busy. They submit inquiries at work, at dinner, between errands. A second follow-up on day two, a value email on day three, and a check-in text on day five keep your business in their inbox without being annoying.

The goal is to be the easiest contractor to reach, not the most aggressive. The shop that shows up consistently in a homeowner's phone with helpful, specific messages is the shop that gets the job when the homeowner is finally ready to talk.

The Recovery Play: When You Miss the Window

Sometimes the 5-minute window slips. A lead comes in at noon and you are mid-shingle. The phone rings and you are on a ladder. The lead goes to voicemail, and the homeowner moves on.

The recovery play is simple: a 60-second missed-call protocol. The moment you see a missed call, send a text acknowledging it, note the number, and follow up as soon as you are off the job. The faster the recovery, the more likely the homeowner is to give you a second chance.

FAQ

Why is a 5-minute response time considered the gold standard for contractors? A 5-minute response ensures you engage the homeowner while their intent is at its peak. Older research from the MIT-affiliated Lead Response Management Study shows that waiting even 30 minutes significantly degrades the chances of connecting with and qualifying a lead (MIT/InsideSales, 2007), and recent contractor-platform data confirms the pattern still holds (Housecall Pro, 2025).

How does AI help contractors maintain a fast response time without manual effort? AI tools can automatically trigger text messages the moment a lead submits a form or leaves a voicemail. This allows a shop to provide an immediate response while the contractor is on a job site, ensuring the lead feels acknowledged without requiring anyone to stop working.

Should I prioritize calling or texting when a new lead comes in? Use both. Send an immediate text to acknowledge the request, then follow up with a phone call within the first 10 minutes. The text provides instant acknowledgment, while the call provides the personal connection needed to book the job.

What is the best way to follow up if a lead doesn't answer the first call? Leave a professional voicemail and send a brief follow-up email or text. Mention that you are following up on their specific request and provide a clear way for them to contact you back when they are ready.

How many times should a contractor attempt to contact a lead in the first 24 hours? A robust strategy includes at least three touches within the first 24 hours: an initial text, a follow-up call, and a value-based email. Persistence matters because many homeowners do not respond to the very first attempt.
