What Leads Should Contractors Respond to First? A Triage Guide for Roofing, HVAC, and Plumbing

Not all leads are created equal. To maximize conversion, prioritize emergency and same-day repair requests for instant response, while qualifying research-stage inquiries within the hour. Speed-to-lead is critical, but context determines whether you drop everything or follow a structured workflow. The 5-15-60 rule gives your shop a practical framework: emergencies in 5 minutes, quote requests in 15, and research queries in 60.

Why Speed-to-Lead Still Wins in Home Services

In the trades, the first shop to answer the phone often wins the job. Older research from the MIT-affiliated Lead Response Management Study (2007) established that lead conversion drops significantly when response times exceed five minutes. While that research is historical, recent industry data from Invoca (2025) continues to validate that speed remains a primary driver of booking success in home services.

When a homeowner has a problem, they are rarely calling just one shop. They are likely cycling through Google Business Profile listings and calling the first three companies that look reliable. If you wait an hour to call back, you are not just missing a lead: you are handing that customer to your competitor who answered in sixty seconds.

The data backs this up. According to call analytics data from Invoca (2025), contractors who respond to inbound calls within the first minute see meaningfully higher booking rates than those who take longer. The same pattern holds across roofing, HVAC, and plumbing: speed signals competence, and homeowners reward it with their business. For a deeper dive on the numbers behind this, see The 5-Minute Rule: Why Speed-to-Lead Beats Marketing Spend.

Emergency and Safety Leads: The Instant Reply Tier

Some inquiries require an immediate, human-led response. These are the leads that keep the lights on and build your reputation for reliability. You should treat these as high-priority, instant-reply events:

  • HVAC: No heat in winter or no cooling during a heatwave.
  • Plumbing: Burst pipes, active leaks, or suspected gas smells.
  • Roofing: Active interior leaks during a storm or significant structural damage.
  • General: Any request explicitly stating the homeowner needs someone on-site today.

These customers are in distress. They are not looking for the cheapest price; they are looking for the fastest relief. According to 2025 data from Housecall Pro, emergency and same-day repair requests convert at the highest rates when the contractor responds within the first five minutes. After that window, the homeowner has usually already booked with the next shop on their list.

The key distinction here is pain level. A homeowner with water pouring through their ceiling is making a decision in minutes, not hours. A homeowner asking about a roof replacement for next spring is making a decision over weeks. Your response time should match their urgency, not your inbox order.

A pattern that shows up consistently in roofing and HVAC shops that track lead intent: emergency and same-day calls often make up fewer than 1 in 5 weekly inbound leads, yet they account for a disproportionate share of weekly revenue. The reason is straightforward. Emergency callers have already decided to hire someone today. They are not comparison shopping across five bids. They are calling until someone picks up, and whoever picks up first gets the job at a premium price point because urgency overrides price sensitivity. Research-stage leads, by contrast, might convert at a fraction of that rate because the homeowner is still gathering information and will likely talk to three or four competitors before committing.

Intent Mapping: How to Categorize Your Incoming Leads

Not every lead needs a phone call the second it hits your inbox. Using an intent map helps your team stay focused without burning out on low-priority research requests. The goal is to sort leads by what the homeowner is actually trying to accomplish, not just by the order they arrived.

Intent Type Examples Priority Why it matters
Emergency Burst pipe, gas smell, no AC Instant Highest pain, shortest decision window
Same-day repair Can you come today? Instant Strong purchase intent
Quote request Water heater replacement Fast High commercial value
Research How much does a furnace cost? Next hour Lower urgency
Vague inquiry Need help, please call back Triage first Unclear urgency, needs qualification

A common pattern in shops that handle high call volume is that vague inquiries can turn out to be emergencies. A homeowner who leaves a message saying "need help, please call back" might have a flooded basement or might just want a quote for next quarter. Treat vague leads as fast-triage: send a one-question text asking whether this is an emergency or a routine request, and route accordingly.

The 5-15-60 Rule: A Practical Response Framework

To keep your shop running efficiently, use the 5-15-60 rule. This framework ensures you never lose a hot lead while keeping your administrative workload manageable. It maps directly to how homeowners behave when they have a problem: the more pain they are in, the faster they book with whoever answers.

  1. The 5-Minute Window: Reserved for emergency and same-day repair requests. If they need help now, call them now. According to Angi (2025), homeowners searching for emergency repairs typically contact three to four contractors and book with the first one that picks up.
  2. The 15-Minute Window: Reserved for specific quote requests. If a homeowner has provided their address and a clear scope of work, they are ready to move. A quick text or call here confirms you are the professional they want. Fifteen minutes is fast enough to beat most competitors but gives your team room to finish the job they are on.
  3. The 60-Minute Window: Reserved for research-stage leads. If someone is asking about the cost of a new roof or the difference between shingle types, they are in the shopping phase. A response within the hour is sufficient to stay top-of-mind without disrupting your field operations.

The framework is simple on purpose. Complex triage systems fail because nobody uses them when the phone is ringing. Three buckets, three time windows, one rule: match your speed to their urgency.

A Worked Example: What 50 Leads Look Like in a Busy Week

(Illustration, not a client result.)

Consider a mid-size roofing and HVAC shop receiving 50 inbound inquiries in a typical week. Without any triage system, all 50 land in the same queue. The dispatcher works through them in order, which means a homeowner with an active leak might sit behind three price-check emails.

Here is what those 50 leads look like when sorted by the 5-15-60 framework:

Tier Lead Count Response Window What Happens
Emergency / same-day 8 5 minutes Direct call from owner or lead tech
Quote request 17 15 minutes Call or text with scheduling link
Research / info 19 60 minutes Email or text with resources and a soft ask
Vague / unclear 6 15 minutes (triage text) One-question filter, then route

The eight emergency leads in this example are the highest-value opportunities of the week. They are also the most time-sensitive. If even two of them slip to the next day because the dispatcher was buried in research queries, that is two jobs lost to whoever picked up the phone first.

Think about the routing logic. An emergency call at 7 AM on a Tuesday about a burst pipe under a kitchen sink should go straight to the on-call tech's cell phone, not into a general inbox. A quote request for a roof replacement that comes in at 2 PM should route to the dispatcher with the address and photos already attached. A research question about furnace efficiency ratings can wait for a templated email sent by the end of the day. The 5-15-60 framework is not just about speed. It is about making sure the right person handles the right lead at the right moment, so your lead tech is not answering price-check emails while an emergency caller gets voicemail.

This is the core insight behind the 5-15-60 rule: lead value and lead urgency are correlated, and both decay fast. The 19 research leads in this example are worth following up on, but none of them will book today. The 8 emergency leads will book today, and most will book with the first shop that answers. Prioritizing accordingly is the difference between a shop that grows and a shop that stays flat. Automate the Handshake, Personalize the Conversation

Trying to handle every single lead manually is a recipe for burnout. The key is to automate the initial acknowledgment so the customer knows they have been heard, then personalize the follow-up based on the intent map.

A common pattern in many shops is using an automated text-back to confirm receipt of a web form or missed call. This satisfies the customer's need for immediate acknowledgment. Once the automated system confirms the inquiry, your team can prioritize the callback based on the urgency tiers defined above. This allows you to maintain a human touch where it matters most, like on an active leak, without getting bogged down by generic price-check emails.

The split is simple: automate the handshake, personalize the conversation. A text that says "Got your message, someone from your team will reach out within 15 minutes" costs nothing to send and buys real time to respond properly. After-Hours Strategy: When Emergencies Cannot Wait

The most expensive lead is the one that sits in your inbox from Friday night until Monday morning. If you are not running a 24/7 dispatch desk, you need an after-hours strategy for true emergencies.

A simple voicemail is often not enough. Many successful shops now use AI-powered answering or specialized after-hours dispatch services to filter emergency calls. If a homeowner calls with a burst pipe at 9 PM, they need to reach a human or a system that can trigger an emergency dispatch. If they get a standard "we are closed" recording, they will simply hang up and call the next name on the list.

The after-hours problem is really a triage problem in disguise. The same 5-15-60 logic applies, but the stakes are higher because no one is in the office to sort leads manually. An after-hours system should do one thing well: identify whether the call is an emergency, and if so, route it to someone who can respond. Everything else can wait for the morning queue. Frequently Asked Questions

Does responding to a lead in under 5 minutes actually increase conversion rates?

Yes. Older research from the MIT-affiliated Lead Response Management Study (2007) established that lead conversion drops sharply after five minutes, and recent 2025 data from Invoca corroborates that faster response times correlate with higher booking rates. Homeowners value the speed of the response as a proxy for the speed of the service.

How should I handle vague leads that do not specify the problem?

Treat these as high-priority triage. Send a quick, templated text asking, "Is this an emergency or a repair request?" This one-question filter allows you to identify if the lead needs an instant call or if it can wait for a scheduled slot.

What is the best way to handle emergency leads that come in after business hours?

Use an AI-powered receptionist or a dedicated after-hours service. The goal is to ensure the customer feels heard and knows that help is on the way, rather than leaving them to wonder if you will ever call back.

Should I treat a request for a new roof estimate the same as an active roof leak?

No. An active leak is an emergency that requires an instant response to prevent property damage. A request for a new roof estimate is a high-value sales opportunity that can be scheduled for a professional consultation within the 15-minute window.

How can I automate my lead response without losing the personal touch?

Use automation for the "handshake" (confirming receipt) and humans for the "conversation" (diagnosing the problem). Automate the initial text-back so the customer knows you are coming, then follow up with a personal call to address their specific needs.
